Chapter 1

Welcome to the Street

“She had a chicken. A whole chicken. In a bag. And she went in and shut the door.” — Pip, age nine months

You are a gang of street cats. You live behind the bins, under the cars and on the warm roofs of the bakery. Tonight one of you saw a human carry something into a house: something delicious, something fun, something shiny, or a box. Now the whole gang wants it. You’ll break in, grab it and leg it — without getting pinched.

Nine Lives Gang is a tabletop roleplaying game for 3 to 5 players and one Storyteller, for ages 8 and up. Each player is one cat in the gang. The Storyteller plays the house: the humans who live there, their pets, and the prize that is about to be eaten, played with or put away. One robbery takes about one to two hours.

What you need

  • Six-sided dice — about ten for the table. A roll never uses more than five.
  • An eight-sided die (d8) for making cats. No d8? Roll a d6 twice and use the table’s note.
  • A cat sheet for each player, the house tracker for the Storyteller and the quick reference for everyone (all at the back of this book), and pencils.
  • A handful of tokens (coins, buttons, cat treats) for Cattitude, and a counter for each cat, human and pet to put on the map.
  • The house you’ll rob — its pages, with the map. Part Four has five ready to play.

How a robbery goes

  1. Make your cats (Chapter 2), or pick from the ready-made gang (Chapter 3).
  2. The human goes in. The Storyteller reads the start of the house: who lives there, and what the human just carried inside.
  3. Pick your ways in. Every house has several entrances — a cat flap, a window left open, the gap behind the human’s legs. Cats can split up.
  4. Play in rounds. Each round starts with the Prize Clock: is the prize being put down, moved or taken away? Then every cat gets one Move and one Action. Then the house moves: the humans wander and the dog sniffs about.
  5. Get it, and get out. Eat it where it is, or get it outside — and get every cat outside too.
  6. The getaway. Leg it over the fences with the loot before animal control pinches you (Chapter 9).
  7. Count the loot that got home: a Clean Getaway, a Messy Getaway, or Busted.

The whole game on one hand

To do something tricky, roll some six-sided dice and look at the highest one. A 6 means it works. A 4 or 5 means it works, but you made a noise. A 1, 2 or 3 means it didn’t work, and you made a noise. Two 6s is Purrfect.

Being seen isn’t being caught. Being seen doing something is. Every time a human sees you do something, their mood fills up — towards the Broom if you’re naughty, towards a Cuddle if you’re sweet. Fill one all the way and a cat gets chased out or scooped up.

Nobody gets hurt

This is a cartoon. Cats always land on their feet, dogs only ever bark and chase, and the broom only ever sweeps a cat out of the door. The worst thing that can happen to a cat in this game is that a human loves it so much they keep it — and it has to live in a warm house with a full food bowl for ever. The gang will miss it terribly.

For the Storyteller

Part Two is the rules, Part Three is how to run them, and Part Four is five houses ready to play. Read Chapter 12 before your first robbery; it has a checklist for every round.

Words to know

WordWhat it means
ApproachHow your cat does something: Sneaky, Sweet, Savage or Streetwise. Each has a rating of 1 to 4.
InstinctSomething your cat can’t help doing, like knocking things off tables. It helps you (its Knack: +1 die when it suits what you do) — and tempts you.
Flop, Scrape, Clean, PurrfectHow a roll went: it didn’t work; it worked noisily; it worked quietly; it worked perfectly (two 6s).
PushReroll every die that isn’t a 6, once, for a price.
CattitudeYour cat’s swagger, kept as tokens. Spend it to Push or to resist a temptation.
AttentionHow hard a human is to fool (0–2). It takes dice off Sneaky and Sweet rolls they watch.
DistractedA human a cat has charmed: they see nobody until the house’s turn.
The MoodHow the household feels about the cats: two meters, Broom and Cuddle.
PatienceHow full a Mood meter can get before something happens: a full Broom means Shoo! (chased out); a full Cuddle means Scooped! (picked up and Held).
DenThe room a human shuts a held cat in. A friend can let it out.
SearchingA human who knows something’s up: they hunt for the prize and the cats instead of following their routine.
Prize ClockWhen the prize is put down and moved, and the round it’s gone (eaten, put away or thrown out). Lights Out comes two rounds later: any cat still inside is pinched.
PinchedCaught by animal control and taken to the pound. In a campaign a cat spends a Life to bust out.
LootWhat the gang got out of the house, in pieces, to carry home in the getaway.
RoundEvery cat takes a turn, then the house does.
StorytellerThe player who runs the house and everyone in it.

Example of play

Storyteller: “Round 3. The chicken has sat on the kitchen counter since last round, still in its foil. Mr Pell is filling the kettle at the sink, humming, and Pudding thumps his tail in his basket. The kitchen window is open above the bins.”

Tilly’s player: “Two-Bin Tilly hops up the bins and slips through the window behind his back. Sneaky!” Tilly has Sneaky 2, and climbing is her Knack (she’s a Climber), but the window is Tricky and Mr Pell is right there (Attention 1): 2 + 1 − 1 − 1 = 1 die. She rolls a 6. A Sneaky Clean: she’s in, and he never noticed. Pudding did — and Tilly has used her Action climbing in, so she’ll have to deal with him first thing next turn.

Big Biscuit’s player: “Biscuit goes through the cat flap into the utility room — it clacks — and hides behind the washing machine.” No roll, but the flap makes a noise. In the house’s turn Pudding barks at Tilly, but Mr Pell answers the first noise: he goes into the utility room to see what clacked, and finds nothing — leaving Tilly alone with the chicken and a very waggy dog…
